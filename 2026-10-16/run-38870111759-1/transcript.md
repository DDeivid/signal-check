## ASML's Record Quarter

**Mia:** We start with the company that sits at the center of the global semiconductor world. ASML released its third-quarter earnings on Thursday, and the numbers are staggering. Revenue hit 13.06 billion dollars, or 11.66 billion euros, which is up 55 percent compared to this time last year. Operating profit climbed to nearly 5.79 billion dollars, and gross margins reached a record 57.5 percent.

**Theo:** It is an eye-popping financial sheet. Net income came in at 5.56 billion dollars, though to be precise, about 570 million of that was an accounting re-evaluation gain on their investment in Mistral AI. But stripped of that, the underlying manufacturing business is firing at full tilt. They shipped 121 lithography systems during the quarter, including 17 extreme ultraviolet, or EUV, machines that accounted for 4.59 billion dollars on their own.

**Mia:** And what is driving this is not normal consumer gadget replacement cycles. It is the unyielding spend on AI data centers. Logic chipmakers represented 54 percent of equipment sales, while advanced memory accounted for 46 percent. ASML's chief executive, Christophe Fouquet, says their leading-edge buyers have unprecedented visibility from their own customers. In fact, ASML says demand will exceed their factory capacity in 2027 and 2028, and some chipmakers are already trying to secure delivery slots for 2029 and 2030.

**Theo:** That is the headline narrative, but as always with ASML, you have to look at the geography and the contractual reality. Taiwan made up 32 percent of shipments and South Korea took 31 percent. That means TSMC and Samsung together account for 63 percent of all equipment revenue.

**Mia:** And the United States?

**Theo:** Just 13 percent, despite all the federal subsidies flowing into domestic fabrication plants. And while Fouquet talks about customers asking for 2030 delivery dates, asking for a slot on a forward horizon plan is not the same thing as signing a binding, non-refundable purchase order.

**Mia:** There is also reported tension behind the scenes over the price tag on these machines. Tom's Hardware highlighted that ASML's planned price hikes on Low-NA EUV tools have sparked pushback from key customers, specifically TSMC. These tools cost hundreds of millions of dollars each.

**Theo:** Naturally. When you hold an effective global monopoly on the only lithography machines that can print advanced microscopic circuits, your clients have to pay whatever you ask.

**Mia:** Even if they do not enjoy it.

**Theo:** Exactly. ASML expects full-year 2026 revenue to land between 49.3 billion and 50.4 billion dollars, with fourth-quarter sales alone projected to reach up to 17 billion. Memory-related equipment sales are projected to surge 75 percent this year, driven largely by high-bandwidth memory for AI accelerators. The supply squeeze is real, but chipmakers are effectively being forced into massive forward commitments just to keep from being locked out of the queue.

## Trump and Ukraine's Refinery Strikes

**Mia:** Turning to Washington and Eastern Europe, where diplomatic relations between the United States and Ukraine have hit an open crisis. In an interview aired Thursday on Newsmax, Donald Trump lashed out at Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, calling Ukraine's renewed strikes on Russian oil refineries a disgrace.

**Theo:** Trump was remarkably blunt. He claimed that hitting diesel refineries makes world oil prices higher and hurts global markets. He argued that diesel is essential, said Zelenskyy is not doing a good service for the world, and said flatly, quote, 'I think I find Zelenskyy to be more difficult to deal with than Putin.' He also repeated that he is not a big fan of the Ukrainian president.

**Mia:** The backdrop here is the total breakdown of what Trump had presented as an energy ceasefire last week. Trump had abruptly announced a truce on strikes against energy infrastructure, but as reporting from The Guardian points out, neither Moscow nor Kyiv ever actually confirmed they had agreed to it. Zelenskyy formally walked away from it after Russia kept attacking Ukrainian infrastructure, including a drone strike that hit energy facilities in the city of Lutsk, causing blackouts.

**Theo:** And look at the economic calculus driving the White House here. Last week, Trump eased US sanctions on Russia after Moscow agreed to release substantial amounts of diesel onto global commodity markets.

**Mia:** Analysts saw that as an attempt to suppress retail fuel prices ahead of the upcoming US midterm elections.

**Theo:** Right. And Zelenskyy condemned that deal in scathing terms, arguing that lifting sanctions hands Moscow the revenue it needs to sustain a war that has dragged on for over four and a half years.

**Mia:** Things became even more fraught when Ukraine reported sudden restrictions on Starlink satellite service, which hobbled Ukrainian drone guidance and air defenses along the front line. During a visit to London, Zelenskyy said those Starlink blackouts made him feel personally betrayed by Trump. British Defence Secretary Wes Streeting said he raised this directly with US Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, who assured him Starlink access had been restored, though Streeting noted the root cause of the outage remains unclear.

**Theo:** Meanwhile, European leaders are visibly trying to construct a diplomatic buffer around Kyiv. NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte met with Zelenskyy in Brussels, French President Emmanuel Macron insisted Ukraine must receive all means necessary to defend itself, and Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk publicly posted, 'YOU ARE NOT ALONE.' But with Zelenskyy attending an EU summit today, European leaders face the reality that diplomatic solidarity cannot easily substitute for American logistical and satellite support.
