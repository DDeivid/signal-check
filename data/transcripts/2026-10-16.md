## Wi-Fi 7 Hardware Hacked into Wideband Radio

**Mia:** Our second main story is a fascinating breakthrough in open-source hardware hacking that could reshape how researchers and radio enthusiasts work with high-frequency spectrum. A developer known as h0m3us3r has released an open-source project called w1700k-sdr, which successfully turns an off-the-shelf Wi-Fi 7 router into a wideband software-defined radio receiver.

**Theo:** For listeners who don't follow the RF world closely, software-defined radio, or SDR, allows you to capture raw radio waves and decode or analyze them using software instead of relying on specialized, fixed analog hardware circuits. The problem has always been that high-bandwidth SDRs are traditionally very expensive lab equipment.

**Mia:** This new project repurposes a commercial Gemtek W1700K Wi-Fi 7 router. It taps into the router's internal MediaTek MT7996 radio chip to capture raw fourteen-bit I/Q radio samples at up to 320 million samples per second. It can do this across all three primary Wi-Fi bands: 2.4 gigahertz, 5 gigahertz, and the newer 6 gigahertz band.

**Theo:** And the technical achievement here isn't just listening to that wide band; it's getting that torrent of data out of the chip. We actually saw an attempt at this just days ago with the ESP-SDR project, which tried turning an ESP32 microcontroller into an SDR receiver and transmitter. But that project relied on a standard UART serial interface to output the data, which choked completely because the serial bus simply couldn't keep up with the sample rate.

**Mia:** Right, UART was a massive bottleneck. But the Gemtek router has hardware designed for modern multi-gigabit wireless routing. The w1700k-sdr project streams the captured raw samples straight over a ten-gigabit Ethernet port using the router's Airoha network components. That provides the massive throughput required to stream 320 million samples per second continuously without dropping packets.

**Theo:** It is undeniably clever engineering. But before everyone runs out to buy a Wi-Fi 7 router as a cheap lab tool, there are still major practical questions. The project writeup leaves out the exact host-side software pipeline needed to ingest and process fourteen-bit I/Q data at ten gigabits per second in real time. Your average laptop cannot easily chew through that data rate without dropping frames or running out of memory.

**Mia:** There's also the question of long-term stability and thermals. Consumer networking gear is built to process packets, but running radio chipsets in continuous raw sample capture mode puts a heavy load on both the silicon and the onboard bus.

**Theo:** Exactly. We'll have to see whether other developers can replicate this on different routers using the same MediaTek MT7996 chipset, and whether someone writes a stable software interface to make this practical for everyday radio monitoring.
